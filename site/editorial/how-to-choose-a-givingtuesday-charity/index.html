How to Choose a GivingTuesday Charity: Seven Questions Worth Asking

Before donating or recommending a nonprofit to your followers on December 1, 2026, a few checks can help you give with more confidence.

By Influencers Involved

Choosing a cause usually begins with a feeling. Perhaps a shelter has looked after an animal you love, or a family member has benefited from a community program. That personal connection matters. It is a perfectly good reason to start looking.

But before you give—or share a donation link with other people—it helps to slow down long enough to understand the organization and the request. The Federal Trade Commission's charity guidance recommends researching charities and checking exactly where donations go.

1. Can I find the organization's official website?

Start with the charity's own website, not just a screenshot or a shortened link in someone else's post. Look for a description of its work, current contact information, and a donation route that clearly identifies the recipient.

An organization may use an outside fundraising platform; that isn't automatically a warning sign. What matters is being able to verify that the organization links to the campaign and that you understand who receives the funds.

2. What does it actually do?

A strong mission statement is useful. A concrete explanation is better.

If an animal welfare group says it rescues pets, does it explain whether it provides shelter, foster placements, veterinary care, adoption services or a combination? If a charity supports students, does it describe the services and communities it works with?

You don't need to become an expert. You should be able to explain the organization's work in a sentence without relying on an emotional slogan.

3. Is the GivingTuesday campaign current?

GivingTuesday 2026 falls on Tuesday, December 1. Check that the campaign page refers to 2026, not an older appeal left online. Look for the date, the request, and any terms around matching gifts or deadlines.

If a charity promises a donation match, ask how it works. Is there a maximum pool? Is it limited to December 1? Are gifts made through every payment method eligible? A campaign may be sound even when its match is limited; your post should reflect the actual terms.

4. Can I verify its charitable status where relevant?

For U.S. organizations, the IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search can help you review exempt status and available filings. Search using the organization's legal name or identifying information; a public brand name and legal filing name do not always match.

Tax-exempt status is not a universal measure of program quality, and rules differ outside the United States. Treat it as one useful check, not a magic seal of approval. If tax deductibility matters to you, verify the specific recipient and your own circumstances rather than assuming every donation qualifies.

5. Does the charity explain how gifts will be used?

A campaign might name a clear need: veterinary treatment, a facility improvement, meals, supplies or training. It may have a broader annual operating appeal. Neither is automatically better.

Ask whether the page makes its purpose understandable. If you are unsure, contact the charity. A thoughtful nonprofit should be able to explain the campaign without pressuring you to donate immediately.

6. Are there signs of pressure or impersonation?

Be careful with unsolicited donation requests, look-alike charity names, unusual payment demands and urgent messages that do not give you time to verify a recipient. The FTC advises checking donation links carefully and warns against requests that insist on gift cards or wire transfers. See its guide to donating safely.

A real GivingTuesday deadline is not a reason to skip basic checks. If you're not comfortable, visit the organization's website independently and start there.

7. Is this an organization I can explain honestly to someone else?

This final question is especially relevant if you plan to post about the charity.

What do you actually know? Have you visited its site, read the campaign, or had personal experience with the cause? Can you separate confirmed facts from your opinion? If someone asks where their contribution goes, can you direct them to the right page?

It's fine to say, "I recently learned about this organization." It's not fine to imply you volunteer there or that a charity approved your post when neither is true.

A practical 10-minute check

Open the charity's official website. Read its mission and the current campaign. Confirm where a donation link leads. Check a reliable independent source, such as the IRS search for a relevant U.S. nonprofit. Look for recent organizational activity. Then decide whether its work fits what matters to you.

If you are a creator, take the extra step of checking any photograph, story or quoted material you want to reuse. Link to the source rather than copying protected content without authorization.

We've assembled a short guide to documented 2026 animal-related GivingTuesday campaigns as one place to practice those checks. It is a starting point, not a ranking of the best charities.

You can be generous without rushing

GivingTuesday is an invitation, not a test. You can decide to donate on December 1, volunteer later, or spend the day learning more about an organization. There is no requirement to make a public declaration.

And if you create content, remember that your audience trusts you for judgment, not just enthusiasm. A careful introduction to a real charity can be a meaningful contribution in itself.
